Theories of Evolution

Natural Selection All species of living things have evolved from simple life forms that first developed more than three billion years ago. This theory was first proposed by the famous Charles Darwin, In his book: 'On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection'. Darwin suggested that populations of species evolve over successive generations through natural selection. Over very, very long periods of time, this could lead to species diverging and forming new species. Evolution is defined as: A change in proportion of the inherited characteristics of a population over time. Natural Selection is defined as: A process where individuals with a phenotype better suited to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Phenotype is defined as: Observable characteristic(s) or trait(s) of an organism. Natural selection has 3 main stages: 1. There is variation in the phenotype between individuals of the same species. 2. Some individuals with a certain phenotype are better suited to the environment and are more likely to survive and breed successfully. The environmental conditions can cause 'selection pressures' such as predation, competition (for a mate), the climate, and pollution, that favours some. 3. The individuals that breed successfully will pass on the more 'favourable' phenotype to their offspring. Over successive generations, this can lead to evolution. There is now more evidence to support this theory (e.g. increased knowledge of genes and DNA) than when it was first proposed, leading to higher acceptance now than initially. Further evidence comes from fossils and the fossil record, depicting such 'favourable' phenotypes. This theory is also shown in speed with bacteria, which reproduce very rapidly.
