Speciation

Alfred Russel Wallace Wallace independently proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection, which matched Darwin's, after his expedition of South America in 1848. Wallace worked worldwide, gathering evidence for his evolutionary theories, for example warning colouration in species. Much of the evidence was pioneering work that contributed to our current understanding of speciation.

Isolation A population may become physically isolated due to geographical conditions such as a new mountain range, river, or an area of land becoming an island. Earthquakes can separate areas of land, and volcanoes can produce new islands. There can also be environmental isolation. For example, if the climate becomes warmer in one area, plants will flower at different times of the year. The breeding times of the plants and animals will change and eventually new species will emerge.

The Process Any population contains natural genetic variation. In each population, the alleles that are selected will control characteristics that help the organism to survive and breed successfully. If a physical barrier (such as a river) geographically separates the population, the two populations can no longer breed. Each population adapts independently to their new environments. Alleles for characteristics that enable organisms to survive and breed successfully in the new conditions will be selected and passed on. Genetic variations between populations will increase and increase, Until, eventually, characteristics change so that they cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring with the other population. This means a new species has formed.
